## Design notes

These are the choices behind `entry.png`, THE THURSDAY LEDGER: a cream,
one-page broadsheet edition for Thursday 19 November 2026. The page brings
40 stories into one view, supported by a stat band, a region tally, and a
date-check footer that distinguishes the day's events from older carryover.
The notes below explain why each design decision was made the way it was,
from the printed-paper framing down to the per-card source credits.

1. **Cream paper, not white.** A pure white page reads as a screenshot or a
   browser window; the warm cream ground (`#faf6ec`-ish) plus a thin black
   rule at the top and bottom of the sheet reads as a printed object. The
   contest asked for a page that sums up a day, and news is an artefact of a
   day - so the page should feel printed and finite, not infinitely
   scrollable.

2. **The red date tab.** The blue "19 NOV 2026" block anchors the masthead,
   but the red band under the hero cards (and the red region chips) exist
   because a newspaper needs a colour hierarchy that works before reading:
   blue is the paper's identity, red is the "this is today, this is
   important" signal. Every red element on the page marks something the
   reader should check first - the hero kicker, the live-source credits,
   the US/Asia chips - so red stays scarce and meaningful rather than
   decorative.

3. **The eight-cell numbers strip.** Eight cells, not six and not twelve,
   because it makes the strip an exact 4x2 of the day's magnitude: two
   court numbers (9-6 ICJ vote, 56-44 Senate split), two money numbers
   ($81bn IPO, $2tn open), one physical event (M5.7), one economic signal
   (3.6% UK inflation), one policy sum (EUR45bn loan) and one sport absurdity
   (6-51). A strip that mixes courts, markets, quakes and a cricket score
   tells you faster than any headline that the page covers the whole planet.
   It also gives the eye a resting row between masthead and heroes.

4. **Ranking into hero / secondary / tertiary / briefs.** Stories were
   ranked by (a) how many regions they touch, (b) whether the event
   *concluded* on 19 November versus merely continued, and (c) whether it
   will still matter in a month. The three heroes are a deliberate
   triangle: one political summit (Shenzhen declaration), one court
   judgment (ICJ genocide ruling), one market event ($81bn IPO) - so the
   top row alone spans politics, law and money. Secondary cards (4-8) are
   consequential but single-region or single-sector. Tertiary cards (9-14)
   carry one fact each and lean on the number, not the prose. The 24
   "Also on Thursday" briefs exist for completeness: any event that
   happened today but wouldn't survive a month still gets a line, region-
   tagged, so a reader scanning for their region always finds it.

5. **The date-check footer.** News pages silently mix "happened today"
   with "still developing from yesterday". The footer makes the cut
   explicit: it lists what the day is *not* (killed-as-not-Thursday claims
   like the Kyiv attack, Gaza push and Myanmar fighting all being
   Wednesday's events) and states the sourcing rule - attributed, never
   asserted, for disputed claims. It exists because the page's whole
   promise is "everything on this page happened or was first reported
   today", and a promise you can't check isn't worth printing.

6. **Grid and density.** 3 + 5 + 6 + 24 stories, plus 8 numbers, from 7
   regions. The descending card sizes (3 wide, 5 medium, 6 small) create a
   pyramid the eye descends naturally; by the time the reader reaches the
   briefs the grid is so regular it can be scanned in columns rather than
   read. Region counts (Asia 8, Europe 8, Americas 8, Africa 6, US 3,
   Oceania 3, Middle East 2) are printed in the tab row so the breadth
   claim is auditable at a glance.

7. **Attribution in the card footer.** Every card ends with a small red or
   blue source credit (REUTERS, ICJ, KYODO, FED...). On a page that
   compresses 38 stories, the credit line doubles as the "who reported
   this" fact-check without needing a separate byline row.
